Where the project stands on 25 August 2026

Offscript

Every scam follows a script. We take you off it.

Offscript is a tool that spots financial scams in India by looking at how a conversation is being steered, not at who is calling. Scammers throw away phone numbers every day. They do not throw away the script, because the script is the part that works.

HackIndia and Appinventiv, Noida, 12 to 13 September 18 days to go 3 of 40 conversations collected 5 scam types mapped No app built yet

Please read this first

There is no app to open yet, and that is on purpose. The hackathon rules say the working software has to be built during the event itself. So what exists today is the groundwork: the collection of real scam conversations, the map of how each scam type behaves, the test system that will score the finished tool, and the helper program for adding new conversations. This page is simply a window onto that groundwork.

The idea

The five steps almost every scam walks through

Blocking a phone number only works until the scammer buys a new one, which takes about a minute. But the steps below stay the same whether the scam arrives as a call or a text, in Telugu or in Hindi, pretending to be the police or your bank. A filter that hunts for suspicious words cannot see a pattern like this. Something that actually reads the conversation can.

0
Nothing harmful here
You speaking, a plain greeting, the header line on a text message. Every single message in a genuine alert sits here, no matter how alarming it sounds.
1
Pretending to be someone official
They say they are the police, your bank, a government office, or customer care. It is delivered calmly and politely, because calm is what makes it believable.
2
Creating a false rush
Two hours before the warrant. Your account closes tonight. A penalty that doubles at five. The rush is the point, because it takes away the one thing that would save you, which is time to check.
3
Cutting you off from help
Do not tell your family. Stay on this video call. The branch is closed today. Sometimes it arrives disguised as safety advice, the kind you already trust, with one small exception carved out for the person on the phone.
4
Making the threat much bigger
Police at your door in thirty minutes with a news crew. Every contact in your phone about to get a message. Your credit score gone. Your daughter's passport cancelled. Fear this size stops people thinking clearly, which is exactly what it is for.
5
Asking for the money
A transfer, a one time password, a PIN, an app that hands over your screen. It is never called a payment. It is called a verification, a refund, a formality, something you will get back in three hours.

A caller says he is a police officer and that a parcel in your name held drugs. He keeps you on video call, tells you not to tell your wife or your son, then warns that police will arrive with the media in thirty minutes. That is where we stop. On stage, Offscript then says what is coming next: he will ask you to transfer your balance to a verification account and promise a refund in three hours. Then we reveal the real ending, and it matches. Notice that the word payment never appears before the black line.

The hardest part of this one is the fifth message. Telling you never to share your one time password sounds like exactly the advice your bank gives, which is why it works. The trick is the small exception at the end: share it with me. We catch the impersonation by checking the web address it sends you to, because that address has nothing to do with the real bank.

This is the message that decides whether anyone would actually keep Offscript installed. It has a deadline, a large amount, and the words block and immediately. A word filter panics. Half of our collection will be genuine messages exactly like this one, and that half is what lets us prove the tool does not cry wolf.

Test results

What the scoring system says today

The scoring system is finished and running. The thing being scored is not: right now a placeholder sits in its place that answers not a scam to everything, which is why every number is zero. These figures prove the test harness works from end to end and nothing more.

Every result carries the number of examples it was measured on. A percentage without that number can mean anything, and it is the first thing a judge will ask about.

Alongside spotting the pattern, Offscript checks the hard facts a scammer states out loud: a helpline number, a web address, a payment ID, a case number. Some of those we can settle for certain. Some we honestly cannot, and we say so rather than guessing.

The difference between those two figures is facts we settled using general knowledge rather than a check the software can run by itself with no internet. Only payment IDs, text message shortcodes, helpline numbers, web addresses and case number formats count toward the lower figure. The lower figure is the one that goes on the slide, because it is the one that survives the follow up question.

Twenty scams spread across all five types and several languages, and twenty genuine urgent messages. This is human work and it cannot be rushed by building more tools, because the tools are already finished. Roughly five or six a day gets it done by the first of September.

Genuine messages containing a checkable fact
At the moment only one genuine message contains something the software can look up and confirm, so that half of the checking is being tested on a single example. That is a story, not a result. Eight is the minimum.
not run
Agreement check between the two of us
Deciding which of the five steps a message belongs to involves judgement, and if two people quietly disagree then every result above becomes meaningless. So one in ten conversations gets marked by both people and the two versions are compared. Worth running around the twenty seventh, not the thirty first: finding a problem after twelve conversations costs twelve redos, finding it after thirty costs thirty.
no source
Text message sender names cannot be verified
Every bank text arrives with a short sender name at the top. There is no public register we can look those up in, so we cannot prove one is real and we cannot prove one is fake. We mark them unknown rather than pretending, and we check the web address instead, which we can prove. This gets said out loud in the pitch, because anyone from the telecom world already knows the register is closed.
